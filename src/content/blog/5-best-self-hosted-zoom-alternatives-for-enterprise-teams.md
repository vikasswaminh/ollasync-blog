There is a particular kind of meeting that makes IT leaders nervous. It is not the weekly standup or all hands. It is the call where someone shares a patient chart, or reads out a term sheet, or walks a client through evidence in an ongoing case. Those are the meetings where the question "where does this data go" stops being theoretical and starts being the first thing procurement asks before anyone signs a contract.
For years, the default answer was Zoom. It is reliable, it is scale, and almost everyone already has it installed. But reliability and scale were never the same thing as sovereignty. Zoom is a multi-tenant cloud product, built and operated by a single vendor, and no matter how many security toggles you flip, your meeting still runs on infrastructure you do not own, in a legal jurisdiction you do not control, subject to terms of service you did not write.
That has become a genuine problem for a specific, growing group of buyers. Hospitals that need every telehealth session to stay inside HIPAA's boundaries. Law firms protecting privileged communications. Banks and investment firms handling material nonpublic information. Government agencies bound by procurement rules that flatly exclude foreign owned cloud vendors. Universities and training providers whose students expect their coursework to stay put. None of these teams are paranoid. They are reading the compliance requirements they already must meet, and realizing that a multi-tenant SaaS video tool, however well engineered, is a structural mismatch.
That mismatch is why "self-hosted Zoom alternative" has become one of the most searched phrases in enterprise software procurement. The trouble is that most of what comes up when you search it is either thin marketing copy or a list of ten tools with no real explanation of what "self-hosted" costs you in practice. This guide is our attempt to fix that. We are going to walk through five platforms that data-conscious enterprise teams are deploying in 2026, explain what each one gets right and where it still asks something of you, and give you a framework for matching the tool to the risk you are carrying.
One quick disclosure before we start: Ollasync is one of the five platforms on this list, and we built it. We have tried to write about it with the same honesty we apply to the other four, including where it is still maturing. If you want the unvarnished, line by line breakdown of what we do and do not claim, our security page is the place for that. Everywhere else in this guide, we are simply trying to help you make a good decision, even if that decision is not us.

## Why Zoom Itself Cannot Really Be Self-Hosted
Before comparing alternatives, it is worth being precise about what we are alternatives to. Zoom occasionally gets asked about on premise deployment, and the honest answer is that Zoom's on premise offering, where it has existed, has historically been a connector or gateway product layered on top of the Zoom cloud, not a way to run the full platform independently on your own servers. The meeting signaling, the account system, the recording pipeline, and increasingly the AI features all live inside Zoom's infrastructure. You can restrict some traffic to route through your network, but you are not removing the company from the trust boundary. You are adjusting where the data touches down, not who ultimately operates the service.
That is not a criticism unique to Zoom. Microsoft Teams, Google Meet, and Cisco Webex are built the same way, as cloud first products with the vendor sitting permanently in the middle of every call. For most organizations that is a perfectly reasonable trade, because the vendor's scale, reliability, and feature velocity are worth more than the marginal control they give up. But for the data-conscious teams this guide is written for, the vendor being permanently in the middle is precisely the risk they are trying to eliminate.
A self-hosted alternative is architected the other way around. The signaling server, the media relay, the recording storage, and ideally the identity system all run on infrastructure that your own team, or a hosting partner you contract directly, controls. You patch it, you scale it, you decide who has root access, and critically, you decide which country the bytes physically sit in, and which legal system can compel access to them. That is a different proposition, and it comes with different responsibilities, which is exactly what we want to walk through for each platform below.

## What "Data-Conscious" Means for a Buying Decision
"Secure" gets thrown around so loosely in this market that it has almost stopped meaning anything. Before comparing tools, it helps to break "data-conscious" down into the specific, checkable things it implies, because different organizations will weigh these differently.
- Deployment control. Can you run the entire stack, not just a piece of it, on infrastructure you operate, whether that is your own datacenter, a private cloud account, or a fully air gapped network with no internet access at all?
- Encryption model, and whether it is the default. Is media protected only in transit to a server that can technically decrypt it, or is it end to end encrypted so that even the  operator of the relay only ever sees ciphertext? And do you have to remember to turn that on, or is it simply how the product behaves out of the box?
- Data residency and jurisdiction. These are two different questions that get conflated constantly. Residency is about geography, which country the server sits in. Jurisdiction is about legal reach, which government can compel the operator to hand data over regardless of where that server physically is. A server in Frankfurt operated by a company headquartered in a country with broad extraterritorial data access laws still carries jurisdictional exposure, even though the residency box is checked.
- Compliance fit. Does the platform give you what you need to satisfy HIPAA, GDPR, SOC 2, FedRAMP, or whatever framework your industry runs on, or does it just claim to be "compliant" without the underlying architecture to back that up?
- Operational burden. Self-hosting is not free. Someone must patch the server, manage TURN and STUN relays for connectivity, monitor uptime, and handle scaling when three hundred people join a town hall at once. A tool that is technically self-hostable but practically unmaintainable is not a real option for most IT teams.
- Feature completeness. Encryption and control matter, but so does whether your team will use the thing. A self-hosted tool that frustrates users into falling back on personal Zoom accounts has solved nothing.
Keep these six in mind as we go through each platform, because none of the five below score identically across all of them, and that is exactly the point.

## 1. Jitsi Meet
Jitsi Meet is, for a lot of engineering teams, the first name that comes to mind when someone says "self-hosted video conferencing," and for good reason. It has been around since 2003 in various forms, it is open source under the Apache license, and 8x8 has kept meaningful engineering investment flowing into it for years.
- What it is. Jitsi Meet is a WebRTC based video conferencing platform built around a few core components: the Jitsi Videobridge, which is the selective forwarding unit that routes media between participants, Jicofo for conference focus and coordination, and Prosody, an XMPP server that handles signaling. You can run the whole stack yourself using the official Docker deployment, and there is a reasonably active community maintaining installation guides for bare metal, Kubernetes, and most major cloud providers.
- Why do data-conscious teams reach for it. Because it is verifiably open source, security researchers and your own engineering team can read the code. There is no black box to trust on faith. Jitsi supports end to end encryption for smaller meetings using the WebRTC Insertable Streams API, which means for calls under a certain participant count, the videobridge itself can be kept out of the plaintext media path. That is a meaningfully strong privacy posture when it is configured correctly.
- Where honesty must come in. Jitsi's end to end encryption mode has real limitations. It historically works best for smaller meetings and depending on the version and configuration you are running, larger calls fall back to encryption in transit to the videobridge rather than full end to end protection. That is not a dealbreaker, transit encryption is still solid, but if your compliance team is expecting "end to end encrypted" to apply uniformly regardless of meeting size, you need to check the specific deployment carefully rather than assuming the marketing headline covers every scenario.
The other honest caveat is operational. Jitsi is free as in the software costs nothing, but running it well at enterprise scale, with reliable TURN relay infrastructure for users behind restrictive corporate firewalls, load balanced videobridges, and monitoring, is a real infrastructure project. Plenty of organizations underestimate this and end up with a self-hosted Jitsi instance that works fine for a twenty-person team and falls over the first time three hundred people try to join a companywide broadcast. If you have a platform engineering team that already runs Kubernetes clusters and enjoys this kind of work, that is a non-issue. If you do not, budget for either hiring that expertise or paying for a managed Jitsi hosting partner.
- Who is best for. Engineering led organizations, open-source advocates, and teams within house DevOps capacity who want maximum transparency and are comfortable owning the operational complexity that comes with it. It is also a common building block that other, more polished products are built on top of, so understanding it helps you evaluate the rest of this list too.

## 2. BigBlueButton
If Jitsi is the engineer's choice, BigBlueButton is the educator's choice, and it earns that reputation.
- What it is. BigBlueButton, often shortened to BBB, is an open-source web conferencing platform built specifically for online learning. It has been developed since 2007 with funding and contribution from universities, and that heritage shows in every design decision. Where a generic video tool bolts a whiteboard on as an afterthought, BigBlueButton was built around the assumption that you need a whiteboard, breakout rooms, polling, a shared notes document, and multiuser annotation working together from day one.
- Why do data-conscious training providers and universities pick it. Institutions that handle student records, especially under FERPA in the United States or equivalent frameworks elsewhere, often cannot accept a scenario where a third-party cloud vendor is processing recordings of classes containing identifiable student information. BigBlueButton, self-hosted on university owned or contracted infrastructure, keeps that entire pipeline, live sessions, recordings, chat transcripts, inside institutional control. Moodle and Canvas both have native BigBlueButton integrations, which matters enormously for adoption, because faculty do not have to leave their existing learning management system to launch a class.
- Where honesty must come in. BigBlueButton's security model is built around access control and transport encryption rather than end to end encryption of media by default. Sessions run through a central server that can, architecturally, access the media stream, which is a very different trust model from a tool built around keeping the server blind. For a university running internal lectures, that is usually an acceptable trade, the goal is keeping data off third party clouds and inside institutional infrastructure, not necessarily making the institution's own IT department unable to see the content. But if your specific use case requires that even your own server operator cannot read the meeting content, BigBlueButton's default architecture is not built for that requirement, and you should look elsewhere on this list.
The other practical consideration is that BigBlueButton, while open source, has a meaningfully complex deployment stack involving multiple services (FreeSWITCH for audio, a custom recording pipeline, and several supporting components), and the official guidance is opinionated about running on specific, well specified virtual machines. It is more turnkey than raw Jitsi in some respects thanks to good installer scripts, but it is still a genuine server to operate, not a plugin you install and forget.
- Who is best for. Universities, corporate training academies, bootcamps, and any organization whose core use case is structured teaching rather than ad hoc business meetings. If breakout rooms, integrated whiteboarding, and LMS integration matter more to you than end to end encryption of every session, BigBlueButton is hard to beat.

## 3. Nextcloud Talk
Nextcloud built its reputation as a self-hosted alternative to Google Drive and Dropbox, and Talk is its answer to the question "now that our files are self-hosted, why is our video conferencing still going through someone else's cloud."
- What it is. Nextcloud Talk is a video, audio, and chat module that runs as part of the broader Nextcloud platform. If your organization already runs Nextcloud for file storage, and a surprising number of privacy conscious enterprises and government agencies in Europe do, adding Talk gives you video calling that lives natively alongside your documents, without introducing a second vendor or a second login system into your stack.
- Why do data-conscious teams like the integration story. The single strongest argument for Nextcloud Talk is not any individual feature, it is the fact that your files, your calendar, your contacts, and now your video meetings all sit inside one self-hosted platform with one identity system and one audit trail. For organizations that have already made the decision to self-host their document storage for GDPR or sovereignty reasons, adding Talk closes an obvious gap rather than opening a new integration project. Nextcloud also supports end to end encryption for one-to-one calls and offers a High-Performance Backend, built on the same underlying media server technology as other WebRTC platforms, for larger group calls at scale.
- Where honesty must come in. Nextcloud Talk's video and audio quality, and its polish as a dedicated conferencing tool, has historically lagged purpose-built video platforms, particularly at higher participant counts or on unreliable networks. It has improved significantly with the High-Performance Backend, but if your primary need is excellent large-scale webinar or town hall experiences, a tool-built video first rather than filing first with video added on may serve you better. There is also a real cost to the High-Performance Backend at scale, since running it well for hundreds of concurrent users again means real server infrastructure, not a lightweight afterthought.
- Who is best for. Organizations already invested in Nextcloud for file storage and collaboration, particularly in Europe where Nextcloud has strong government and public sector adoption, who want video conferencing to be one more self-hosted module rather than a separate vendor relationship entirely.

## 4. Matrix and Element Call
Matrix occupies a slightly different category from the other tools here, because Matrix itself is not a product, it is an open protocol, and that distinction matters enormously for a data-conscious buyer.
- What it is. Matrix is an open standard for decentralized, end to end encrypted real time communication, originally built for messaging and later extended to voice and video through Element Call, which is built on the Matrix RTC framework. Because it is a protocol rather than a single vendor's product, you are not locked into one company's roadmap or business model. You can run your own Matrix homeserver, most commonly Synapse or the newer, faster Dendrite, entirely on your own infrastructure, and your organization's communications never have to leave a server you control.
- Why do data-conscious teams, especially government and defense adjacent organizations, take Matrix seriously. Matrix has a unusual pedigree for this space: it has been adopted by parts of the French government, the German armed forces, and other public sector bodies specifically because it is an open, federated, auditable protocol rather than a proprietary black box. End to end encryption is a core design principle of the protocol itself, not a bolted-on feature, using the same category of cryptographic ratchet that underpins other serious, encrypted messaging systems. For an organization whose threat model includes nation state level adversaries, that pedigree and that open standard status carries real weight.
- Where honesty must come in. Element Call, the video and voice layer built on Matrix, is younger and less battle tested than the messaging side of the protocol, which has been in serious production use for close to a decade. Group video calls at scale through Matrix have historically been more complex to get right than dedicated video first platforms, and the ecosystem, while improving rapidly, has not always matched the raw video and audio polish of tools like Zoom or even Jitsi for the specific use case of "large, high production value webinar." Running your own homeserver also means owning federation configuration, key backup strategy, and a different operational model than a typical single tenant video server, which asks more of your team than any other option on this list except possibly raw Jitsi at full scale.
- Who is best for. Government agencies, defense adjacent organizations, and any team whose primary requirement is an open, protocol level guarantee of end-to-end encryption with no single vendor able to unilaterally change the rules. If your threat model is sophisticated and your patience for young software is high, Matrix and Element Call deserve serious evaluation.

## Comparing the Five Without Pretending There Is One Right Answer
Reading through those five descriptions, a pattern should be emerging: there is no single best self-hosted Zoom alternative, there is a best fit for your specific combination of risk, existing infrastructure, and team capacity.
  1.If your organization already has a platform engineering team running Kubernetes and wants to read and audit every line of the code your video meetings run on, Jitsi Meet gives you that transparency at the cost of you owning the entire operational burden yourself.
  2.If your core use case is teaching, structured classes with breakout rooms and shared whiteboards, and your data concern is primarily keeping student or trainee records off third party clouds rather than making your own IT team unable to see session content, BigBlueButton is purpose built for exactly that job.
  3.If you have already committed to Nextcloud for document storage and want to avoid introducing a second self-hosted vendor relationship for video, Nextcloud Talk closes that gap cleanly, with the caveat that video polish at very large scale is not its strongest suit.
  4.If your threat model includes sophisticated adversaries and your organization values open protocol level guarantees over any single vendor's promises, and you have the patience for a younger video layer, Matrix and Element Call are the most philosophically rigorous option on this list.
And if you want self-hosting as the default architecture but still expect the product experience, [live translation](https://ollasync.com/features/ai), automatic notes, [confidential deal rooms](https://ollasync.com/features/deal-rooms), that a modern SaaS tool would give you, that is precisely the gap we built [Ollasync](https://ollasync.com) to fill, with the specific honest caveats about our current encryption scope noted above.
None of these five tools score perfectly across every one of the six criteria we laid out earlier, deployment control, default encryption, data residency and jurisdiction, compliance fit, operational burden, and feature completeness. That is not a flaw in this guide, it is the actual shape of the market in 2026. Anyone telling you one tool wins every category is not being straight with you.

## What Self-Hosting Costs You in Practice
It is worth pausing on something the marketing copy for every self-hosted tool, including our own, tends to underweight self-hosting is a real operational commitment, not a checkbox you tick once during setup.
Someone on your team needs to own patching. Video conferencing software, like any internet facing service, has a steady stream of security updates, and a self-hosted deployment that falls behind on patches is arguably a worse security posture than a well-maintained cloud product. Someone needs to own capacity planning, because a self-hosted media relay that works beautifully for fifty concurrent users can fall over entirely at three hundred if nobody has load tested it. Someone needs to own the TURN and STUN relay infrastructure that lets people behind restrictive corporate firewalls connect, which is a surprisingly common source of "why can't I join the call" support tickets in self-hosted deployments. And someone needs to own incident response, because when something breaks at two in the morning before a board meeting, there is no vendor support line to call, there is whoever is on your team's pager rotation.
None of this is a reason to avoid self-hosting. It is a reason to go in with your eyes open about what you are signing your team up for, and to be honest internally about whether you have, or are willing to build, the operational capacity to run this well. For some organizations, a managed hosting partner for one of these open source tools, or a vendor like us that treats self-hosted deployment as a supported product rather than a raw open source project you assemble yourself, is the more realistic path than a fully do it yourself deployment.

## A Practical Framework for Choosing
Instead of asking "which of these five is the best," a more useful question is to score each candidate against your own specific requirements, weighed for your actual risk. Here is a simple way to do that.
  1.Start by naming the specific data category you are protecting. Is it protected health information under HIPAA, privileged legal communications, material nonpublic financial information, government classified or controlled unclassified information, or student records under FERPA? Each of those has different regulatory teeth, and the honest answer for a company handling casual internal meetings is different from one handling any of the above.
  2.Next, ask whether your requirement is about keeping a third-party cloud vendor out of the picture, or specifically about making sure even your own IT team cannot access content. Those sound similar but lead to very different architecture choices, the first can often be satisfied by any of the five tools above once self-hosted, the second narrows you toward end to end encrypted by default options for the specific content you are worried about.
  3.Then look at your team's operational capacity. Do you have engineers comfortable running Kubernetes and reading source code, or do you need something closer to a supported product with a vendor you can call? That single answer probably eliminates two or three of the five options immediately, in either direction.
  4.Finally, check jurisdiction, not just residency. Ask where the vendor is legally headquartered, not just where the servers physically sit, and understand what legal processes could compel access to your data as a result. This is the step buyers skip most often, and it is often the one that matters most for cross borders, sovereignty sensitive organizations.
Once you have honest answers to those four questions, the five options above tend to sort themselves cleanly, and you can go deeper on the one or two that survive the filter rather than trying to evaluate all five in equal depth.

## Migrating Without Breaking Everything at Once
If you have decided to move off a cloud platform toward one of these self-hosted options, resist the temptation to flip the switch for the entire organization on day one. The teams that migrate successfully almost always run a pilot first, typically with the specific department or use case that has the strongest compliance driver, a legal team, a clinical group, or a specific class of regulated meetings, rather than trying to convert every single meeting across the whole company simultaneously.
Run that pilot for at least a full month, long enough to hit a real range of meeting sizes, network conditions, and edge cases like someone dialing in from a hotel wifi network or a corporate firewall that blocks unusual ports. Collect specific, concrete feedback rather than general impressions, did calls connect reliably, was audio and video quality acceptable, did recordings and notes get where people expected them to go. Only after that pilot has run cleanly should you plan a broader rollout, and even then, a phased rollout by department tends to surface problems in a manageable way that a companywide flip of a switch does not.
It is also worth being honest with your users about why the change is happening. A migration that arrives as an unexplained mandate breeds workarounds, people quietly falling back to personal Zoom links because the new tool feels unfamiliar. A migration explained clearly, here is specifically what data category this protects and why it matters for our compliance obligations, tends to get genuine buy in rather than grudging compliance.

Zoom, Teams, Meet, and Webex are excellent products for many meetings that happen every day, and nothing in this guide is meant to suggest otherwise. The case for a self-hosted alternative is specific, not universal: it applies to the meetings, messages, and documents where the question of whose cloud this runs on is itself the risk you are trying to manage.
